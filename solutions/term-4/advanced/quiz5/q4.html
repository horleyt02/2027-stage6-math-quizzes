Problem

The graph of \( y = f'(x) \) is shown below.

For what values of \( x \) is the function \( f(x) \) increasing?

Worked Solution

Step 1: The Condition for an Increasing Function

A function \( f(x) \) is strictly increasing when its rate of change (first derivative) is strictly positive:

\[ f(x) \text{ is increasing} \iff f'(x) > 0 \]

Because the given graph is already the derivative \( y = f'(x) \), we look for where the graph lies strictly above the \( x \)-axis (where \( y > 0 \)).

Step 2: Highlight Regions Above the Axis

Examining the \( x \)-intercepts of \( y = f'(x) \) at \( x = -6, 1, 5 \):

• Between \( x = -6 \) and \( x = 1 \), the curve is strictly above the axis (\( f'(x) > 0 \)).

• For \( x > 5 \), the curve climbs infinitely above the axis (\( f'(x) > 0 \)).

Step 3: Stationary Points & Interval Notation

At \( x = -6, 1, 5 \), we have \( f'(x) = 0 \). At these exact points, the function is stationary, not increasing.

\[ x \in (-6, 1) \cup (5, \infty) \]

Therefore, we use open round brackets \( (-6, 1) \) rather than closed square brackets.

\( (-6, 1) \cup (5, \infty) \)

Therefore, Option B is correct.
