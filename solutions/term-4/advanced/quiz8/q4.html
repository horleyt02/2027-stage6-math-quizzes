Problem

The graph of \( y = g(x) \) is shown.

At which point is the graph of \( y = g(x) \) NOT differentiable?

Worked Solution

Step 1: Define Differentiability Graphically

For a function to be differentiable at a given point, it must be continuous and have a defined tangent line at that point.

Graphically, a derivative represents the slope (gradient) of the tangent line. A function is not differentiable where there are:
  • Sharp corners or cusps
  • Discontinuities (breaks in the graph)
  • Vertical tangent lines (because a vertical line has an undefined slope)

Step 2: Analyse Points A, B, and C

Let's look at the tangent lines (the "steepness") at the first three points.

Point A: The curve reaches a maximum here. The tangent line is perfectly horizontal. A horizontal line has a slope of 0. Since 0 is a defined number, it is differentiable here.

Points B & C: The curve is smooth and sloping downwards. We could draw standard tangent lines with negative slopes here. It is differentiable at both points.

Step 3: Analyse Point D

Now look at Point D, where the curve hits the \(x\)-axis.

At point \( D \), the curve goes straight down into the \(x\)-axis. If we were to balance a tangent ruler against the curve at this exact boundary point, the ruler would be perfectly vertical.

The slope of a vertical line is undefined (\( \infty \)).
Because the slope is undefined, the derivative does not exist.

Point \( D \)

Therefore, Option D is the correct answer.
